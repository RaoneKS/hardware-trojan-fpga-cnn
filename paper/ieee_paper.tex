\title{Runtime Hardware Trojan Detection and Regional Localization in an INT8 FPGA-CNN Accelerator}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Jeevan K. S.}\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Electronics and Communication Engineering\\IIIT Dharwad, India}}

\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a reference-assisted runtime monitor for an INT8 LeNet-style CNN accelerator and five controlled Hardware Trojan variants targeting Conv2 arithmetic, weight/data path, feature interconnect, spatial routing, and control sequencing. The monitor does not consume Trojan-specific activation flags: it compares sampled MAC products with a reference product, checks the expected Conv2 feature address, and flags the T5 stall state. The clean reference also runs the monitor to measure false alarms. The canonical 60-row cycle-accurate simulation matrix (ten MNIST workloads across Healthy/T1--T5) passed with TP=50, TN=10, FP=0 and FN=0, with correct coarse regional localization on 50/50 Trojan rows. The 95% Wilson intervals are 92.9--100% for TPR (50/50) and 0--27.8% for FPR (0/10), illustrating the small sample. Monitor detection latency is 149,137 cycles for T1--T3, 149,135 for T4, and 149,136 for T5 at 50 MHz. The 300-row held-out matrix, Quartus resource/power reports, and DE10 board observations belong to the pre-monitor RTL revision and are not claimed as validation of the new monitor. Fresh synthesis and physical validation of the monitor-enhanced RTL remain future work.
\end{abstract}

hardware Trojan, FPGA security, CNN accelerator, runtime detection, regional localization, INT8, MNIST

\section{Introduction}
Hardware Trojans are malicious hardware modifications that may remain dormant or preserve externally visible behavior on selected tests. Classical work identifies the difficulty of detecting small or rarely activated modifications using conventional verification alone \cite{chakraborty2009hardware}. Neural-network accelerators introduce additional attack surfaces because repeated MACs, memories, interconnects and control paths can be modified while the final class may remain unchanged for selected inputs \cite{clements2018hardware}. A survey of DNN hardware security similarly identifies Trojan, side-channel and fault-injection threats as important security dimensions \cite{mittal2021survey}.

This work evaluates a reference-assisted monitor against five controlled RTL perturbations. Contributions are: (1) an INT8 LeNet-style reference accelerator; (2) five controlled Trojan variants; (3) independent monitor checks for MAC-result consistency, feature-memory address consistency, and the T5 stall state; (4) a 60-case regression that measures both detections and healthy false alarms; and (5) explicit separation of current simulation evidence from legacy Quartus/board evidence.

\section{Related Work}
Prior work relevant to this study falls into four groups. First, neural-network and CNN accelerator Trojan attacks show that internal feature maps, arithmetic and reconfigurable interconnects can be manipulated while preserving selected output behavior. Clements and Lao introduced neural-network Trojan attacks, while FeSHI studied feature-map-level stealthy attacks; more recent work considers reconfigurable FPGA-CNN interconnections and PUF-based countermeasures \cite{clements2018hardware,odetola2021feshi,hou2024interconnect}. A two-level protection scheme and hardware-resilient CNN designs pursue mitigation through architectural protection or redundancy rather than the regional runtime monitor studied here \cite{liu2024twolevel,sun2022resilient,sun2025resilient}.

Second, runtime and anomaly-based approaches monitor circuit behavior or functional relationships. Changepoint-based detection and reverse-function runtime monitoring provide relevant precedent for runtime detection, but they do not evaluate the same five-region INT8 FPGA-CNN campaign used here \cite{elnaggar2019changepoint,mohd2021rmvrf}. Third, structural localization methods use netlist/RTL graph information or gate-level learning. Golden-reference-free graph-convolutional localization and TrojanSAINT illustrate structural approaches; explainable LUT-level localization advances fine-grained diagnosis but does not provide the same on-board runtime regional code \cite{yasaei2022localization,lashen2023trojansa,su2025lutlocalization}. Fourth, lightweight secure accelerator architectures, including Nacc-Guard, seek to reduce attack impact through design-level defenses \cite{li2023naccguard}.

The distinction claimed here is a narrow, reference-assisted functional monitor integrated into a CNN RTL simulation flow. The monitor-enhanced revision is currently validated in simulation; previously archived Quartus and DE10 observations are from the pre-monitor RTL and are not used to claim hardware overhead or physical validation for the current monitor. Direct numerical ranking is inappropriate because the cited studies use different datasets, threat models, attack payloads, hardware platforms, and metrics.

\section{Architecture and Threat Model}
The reference network accepts 28$\times$28 grayscale MNIST images and contains Conv1, ReLU, Pool1, Conv2, ReLU, Pool2, a fully connected classifier and ArgMax. The computation is
\begin{equation}
Y=\operatorname{ArgMax}(f_{FC}(f_{Pool2}(f_{Conv2}(f_{Pool1}(f_{Conv1}(X)))))).
\end{equation}
The attacker is assumed to have RTL/netlist-level access during an untrusted design or synthesis stage. The controlled campaign includes arithmetic corruption (T1), weight-operand bit flip (T2), feature-operand bit flip (T3), source-routing alteration (T4), and a one-cycle control stall (T5).

The monitor checks (i) a sampled post-payload Conv2 MAC product against a separately sampled reference product computed from the original operands, (ii) the registered Conv2 feature address against the address implied by the operation counters, and (iii) the T5 stall state. The public detector/localization outputs are driven by the monitor, not the Trojan-specific t*_detected flags. The two-bit code is 00=no monitored anomaly, 01=PE arithmetic signature, 10=unique weight-bit-flip signature, and 11=feature operand/data path, routing, control, or ambiguous signature. Code 11 does not distinguish T3, T4 and T5.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
Cycle-accurate Verilog simulation uses Icarus Verilog. A dedicated unit test covers healthy behaviour, T1/T2/T3 arithmetic signatures, address/control anomalies, and reset/re-arm. The canonical matrix contains ten workloads (digits 0--9) and six targets, giving 60 rows. The clean reference core also instantiates the monitor, so its ten rows measure false alarms rather than hard-coding them to zero. The FPGA target is the Intel Cyclone V SoC 5CSXFC6D6F31C6 on the Terasic DE10-Standard. The synchronous-M10K reference latency is 634,281 cycles at 50 MHz.

\section{Results}
The canonical simulation ledger contains 10 Healthy and 50 Trojan rows. Therefore TP=50, TN=10, FP=0 and FN=0:
\begin{equation}
TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad F1=2\frac{Precision\cdot TPR}{Precision+TPR}.
\end{equation}
For the monitor-enhanced canonical matrix, $(TP,TN,FP,FN)=(50,10,0,0)$, so the point estimates are $(TPR,FPR,Precision,F1)=(1,0,1,1)$. Using Wilson score intervals, TPR is 92.9--100% (50/50) and FPR is 0--27.8% (0/10). These intervals emphasize that the small controlled sample does not establish population-wide performance. The separate 300-row held-out matrix was run on the pre-monitor RTL and is excluded from claims about the independent monitor; it must be rerun against this revision.

T1 is localized to region 01 for 10/10 workloads, T2 to region 10 for 10/10, and T3--T5 to shared region 11 for 30/30. Regional localization accuracy is therefore 50/50=100\% at the defined resolution.

At 50 MHz, the monitor-facing ledger records T1--T3 detection latency of 149,137 cycles (2.98274 ms), T4 of 149,135 cycles (2.98270 ms), and T5 of 149,136 cycles (2.98272 ms). Healthy/T1--T4 inference is 634,281 cycles; T5 is 634,282 cycles.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The monitor-enhanced RTL detects all 50 controlled Trojan rows and produces no alerts on ten healthy rows in the canonical simulation matrix. This is a result for the selected, known perturbations—not a universal Trojan-detection guarantee. The sample is small, the held-out 300-row campaign has not been rerun on this monitor, and no monitor-enhanced Quartus resource/timing report or physical DE10 validation is yet available. The monitor assumes trusted clock/reset, operation counters, sampled reference operands, and monitor logic; a Trojan that corrupts these trusted inputs may evade it. Code 11 remains a shared coarse region.

\section{Conclusion}
A reference-assisted runtime monitor was integrated into the INT8 FPGA-CNN RTL and passed the canonical 60-case simulation regression plus dedicated unit tests. It detected all 50 selected Trojan cases, raised no alerts on ten healthy cases, and localized the anomalies at the defined coarse resolution. The earlier Quartus and physical-board evidence belongs to the pre-monitor RTL. Fresh synthesis, held-out evaluation, and physical validation are required before making claims about monitor hardware overhead or on-board effectiveness.
